The Buyer's Problem

The $120 Decision

How do you spend your $120 to get the most bang for your buck?

Part 1, your choice

You just got a $120 bonus from your job

Your employer just paid you a $120 bonus. It's yours to spend however you like, and you've had your eye on a few things. Each item below has its price listed next to it. You can buy one of them, or more than one if the total fits within $120. You can also keep some of the money. This activity will help you understand some of the reasons why buyers decide to purchase a given item. An important idea is that sometimes we value a good or service more than the price. We would be willing to pay more than what's on the price tag. This helps us understand how much we value that item.

A band you like is playing in town next month.

Your current pair works, but only in one ear.

Question 2
Model answer

The concert just isn't worth $100 to Omar. The most he would pay is $80, so paying $100 would leave him worse off. Money wasn't the problem. Denise had the same $120 and bought the ticket gladly, because it's worth $160 to her. Same budget, same price, different choice. What separated them was how much each of them values the concert.

What would happen if the bonus was only $80?

Suppose Denise's bonus was $80 instead of $120. Everything else stays the same. The concert is still worth $160 to her, and the prices haven't changed.

Question 3
Model answer

No. Denise is still willing to pay $160 for the ticket, but she isn't able to pay $100 when she only has $80. The concert is now out of reach, and so are the shoes ($90), which weren't worth their price to her anyway. The earbuds cost $60 but are only worth $50 to her. Dinner costs $40 and is worth $60 to her, so dinner is her best option. She would likely buy dinner and keep $40. How much she values each item didn't change at all. Her smaller bonus changed what she could choose from.

Question 4, look back at your own choice
Model answer

Answers will vary. If you chose the concert, the shoes, or earbuds plus dinner, $80 no longer covers it, so your choice has to change even though your tastes haven't. You'd move to the best option still in reach, such as earbuds or dinner alone. If you chose something that costs $80 or less, a smaller budget may not change anything. A budget only changes your decision when it rules out something you would otherwise have picked.

Part 3, name what you just did

Every buyer solves the same problem

You, Denise, and Omar all worked through the same two questions. What would I enjoy most? What can I afford? Economists have names for each part.

Tastes (preferences)

How much you value different things. One way to see your tastes is the most you'd be willing to pay. Tastes differ from person to person, which is why Denise and Omar chose differently.

Budget constraint

The limit on what you can buy, set by how much money you have and what things cost. With $120, any combination costing more than $120 is off the table, no matter how much you want it.

The Buyer's Problem

Choosing the combination you value most from among all the combinations you can afford. Tastes tell you what you'd like. The budget constraint tells you what's possible.

Willing and able are two different things

"Willing to pay" comes from your tastes. "Able to pay" comes from your budget. With $80, Denise was willing to pay $160 for the concert but wasn't able to pay $100. A buyer needs both before a purchase happens.

What about the money left over?

Keeping money is a choice too. Money you don't spend today can go toward something else later, so it isn't wasted. But having more left over doesn't make an option better. Omar could have kept $60 by buying only the earbuds, but he would have given up a dinner he valued at $70 for just $40. The goal is to get as much value as possible from the money you have.
