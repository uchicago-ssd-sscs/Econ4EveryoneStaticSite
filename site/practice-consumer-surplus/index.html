Economics, Consumer Surplus

Practice Calculating Consumer Surplus

Work out how much better off buyers are than the price alone suggests.

Competitive markets help bring value to buyers, and economists measure that value with the concept of consumer surplus.

Key ideas

Willingness to pay

The most a buyer would be willing to pay for one unit of a good. It is what the good is worth to that person.

Price paid

What the buyer actually pays. In a market, everyone who buys pays the same price.

Consumer surplus

Willingness to pay minus the price paid. It is the extra value a buyer gets from the deal.

One rule to remember. A person only buys if their willingness to pay is higher than the price. Someone who does not buy gets a consumer surplus of $0. It is never negative, because they simply walk away.

Model answer

When the price falls from $5 to $3, every buyer who was already in the market keeps buying but pays $2 less per latte. That is a $2 gain for Ava, Ben, and Carlos, or $6 in total, even though they would have bought anyway.

The lower price also brings in a new buyer. Dana was willing to pay $4, which was below the old price of $5, so she sat out. At $3 she buys and gets a surplus of $1. Add the two effects and total consumer surplus rises by $7, from $9 to $16.

Buyers gain when prices fall because they keep more of the value they receive, and because more people find the purchase worthwhile.

In summary

Consumer surplus is the difference between what buyers are willing to pay and what they actually pay, added up across all buyers. It measures the value buyers gain from a market. In an efficient market, trades that are worth more to buyers than they cost to produce go ahead, which creates as much total surplus as possible. Consumer surplus is the buyers' share of that total, and the sellers' gains make up the rest.
